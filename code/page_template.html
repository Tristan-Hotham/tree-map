What grows where

Is there a map of tree types across the UK, and the world, that you can simply explore? This is my attempt: every mapped wood in Great Britain by type, the species and planting years of England's public forests, and where 88 tree genera turn up in the world's plant records.

Trees are everywhere in Britain and yet surprisingly hard to see as a whole. The Forestry Commission maps every wood of half a hectare or more from aerial photographs, the National Forest Inventory, and publishes it as open data. I took all {{nfi.polygons|int}} of its 2024 map polygons, the species and planting year of every one of the {{fe.subcompartments|int}} compartments in England's public forests, and the records of 88 tree genera from GBIF, the world's largest open archive of where living things have been seen. Then I put them on one map.

Start with Great Britain: zoom in until you can see single woods, switch to the summary of cover and mix, or pick a tree and see where it is recorded. Then switch to the world. Use "Map layers" to change the base map, add hills, place names and borders, and turn the colours up or down so you can always see where you are.

    03 · Three countriesScotland's conifers, England's broadleaves

    Woodland covers {{gb.woodland_pct|f1}}% of Great Britain's land, {{gb.woodland_kha|int}} thousand hectares of it. The three countries are very different. Scotland has {{nations.Scotland.share_of_gb_woodland_pct|int}}% of Britain's woodland on a third of its land, and {{nations.Scotland.conifer_share_pct|int}}% of its broadleaf and conifer woodland is conifer. England's woods are mostly broadleaved: only {{nations.England.conifer_share_pct|int}}% is conifer. Wales sits between, at {{nations.Wales.conifer_share_pct|int}}%.

    Woodland as a share of land, by type
    National Forest Inventory 2024, woodland of 0.5 ha or more. Land area from ONS boundaries.
    Hover a block for its area. "Felled and restocking" is woodland mapped as felled, being prepared for planting, windblown or failed when the photographs were taken.

    About a fifth of all woodland ({{gb.groups_pct_of_woodland.felled|int}}%) was mapped as felled or being prepared for replanting, most of it in Scotland, where it is {{nations.Scotland.felled_pct_of_woodland|int}}% of the woodland. That is the plantation cycle at work: conifer crops are clear-felled and replanted, and newly planted ground can look bare from the air for some years, so some of this land has probably been replanted already.

    04 · The wooded and the bareMoray to Shetland, Kielder to the Fens

    Of the {{counties.n|int}} counties and council areas, Moray is the most wooded, at {{counties.most.0.woodland_pct|f1}}%, closely followed by South Ayrshire, Argyll and Bute, and Dumfries and Galloway, all around 30% and all mostly conifer. England's most wooded county is Surrey ({{counties.by_nation.England.most.woodland_pct|f1}}%), and its woods are {{counties.by_nation.England.most.conifer_share_pct|int}}% conifer. In Wales it is West Glamorgan ({{counties.by_nation.Wales.most.woodland_pct|f1}}%). The barest are the islands: Shetland has {{counties.least.0.woodland_pct|f2}}% woodland, Orkney {{counties.least.1.woodland_pct|f2}}% and the Western Isles {{counties.least.2.woodland_pct|f1}}%. In England, Cambridgeshire ({{counties.by_nation.England.least.woodland_pct|f1}}%) and the East Riding of Yorkshire are the least wooded.

    05 · What the records sayPine by the Spey, beech at Savernake

    The National Forest Inventory says what kind of wood is where, but not which trees. For that I turned to GBIF, which gathers records of where species were seen or collected, from herbarium sheets to phone apps and national surveys. In Great Britain there are {{gbif_gb.plant_records|int}} records of vascular plants in the grid I used ({{gbif_gb.cell_deg|f2}} degree cells, about 10 by 6 km). Pick "Recorded trees" on the map and choose a tree.

    The patterns are recognisable. The share of records that are pines is highest in Strathspey and the northern Cairngorms, in the cell about {{gbif_gb.genera.Pinus.top.0.km|int}} km south east of Nethy Bridge ({{gbif_gb.genera.Pinus.top.0.share_pct|f1}}% of all its plant records). This is the country of Abernethy and Rothiemurchus, the largest remnants of the native Caledonian pinewood. Juniper, a shrub of those pinewoods, peaks in the next cell to the south west ({{gbif_gb.genera.Juniperus.top.0.share_pct|f1}}%), and birch in the cell south of that, towards Braemar ({{gbif_gb.genera.Betula.top.0.share_pct|f1}}%). Beech peaks at Wootton Rivers beside Savernake Forest in Wiltshire ({{gbif_gb.genera.Fagus.top.0.share_pct|f1}}%), ash on the edge of Swindon, and oak in the cell around Long Ditton in Surrey. Within 10 km of Abernethy Forest, {{named.abernethy.woodland_pct|int}}% of the land is woodland, {{named.abernethy.conifer_share_pct|int}}% of it conifer.

    Records are not trees, and the map shows it. Willows are the most recorded tree genus in most British cells ({{gbif_gb.dominant_cells.0.1|int}} of {{gbif_gb.cells_ok|int}} cells with {{gbif_gb.min_records|int}} or more records), because there are many willow species and botanists record them carefully, not because willow is our commonest tree. And where one big survey has been shared, it can swamp a cell: around Bala in Gwynedd, spruce makes up {{gbif_gb.genera.Picea.top.0.share_pct|int}}% of all plant records. That is why I map shares of plant records, not counts, and fade cells with few records; even so, read the "Recorded trees" layer as a map of what botanists have noted, not a census.

    06 · The public forestsWhat was planted when

    Forestry England manages the public forests of England, and it publishes every compartment with its main species and the year the current trees were planted. Its {{fe.subcompartments|int}} compartments cover {{fe.area_ha|int}} hectares, of which {{fe.species_ha|int}} carry a recorded species. {{fe.conifer_pct|int}}% of that is conifer: Sitka spruce alone is {{fe.top_species.0.2|f1}}%, Corsican pine {{fe.top_species.1.2|f1}}% and Scots pine {{fe.top_species.2.2|f1}}%. Oak is the biggest broadleaf, at {{fe.top_species.3.2|f1}}%, then beech at {{fe.top_species.4.2|f1}}%. Tick "Public forest species" on the map and zoom in to see each compartment as a dot.

    This is not the history of planting, because when a crop is felled and replanted, its compartment takes the new date. So the trees standing today were mostly planted from the 1950s on, and conifer planting peaks in the {{fe.conifer_peak_decade|raw}}s, the restocking of the great post-war plantations. What the chart does show is the change in what goes in: broadleaves were {{fe.broadleaf_pct_1950s|int}}% of the area planted in the 1950s that still stands, {{fe.broadleaf_pct_1960s|int}}% in the 1960s and {{fe.broadleaf_pct_1990s|int}}% in the 1990s, then {{fe.broadleaf_pct_2000s|int}}% in the 2000s and {{fe.broadleaf_pct_2010s|int}}% in the 2010s. Sitka spruce is {{fe.periods.1980_1999.0.1|int}}% of the area standing from plantings in 1980 to 1999 and {{fe.periods.2000_2029.0.1|int}}% of plantings since 2000.

    07 · The worldPines, oaks, figs and eucalypts

    Switch the map to "The world". GBIF holds {{world.plant_records|int}} records of vascular plants, and I fetched the records of {{world.genera_n|int}} tree genera, from oaks and pines to baobabs, mahoganies and mangroves. The first map asks which of those genera is most recorded in each {{world.cell_deg|f2}} degree cell with at least {{world.min_records|int}} plant records. It is dominated by big, much-recorded genera: figs over {{world.dominant_area.0.2|int}}% of the qualifying land, mostly in the tropics; willows over {{world.dominant_area.1.2|int}}%, mostly in northern Europe; oaks over {{world.dominant_area.2.2|int}}%.

    So the default map asks a fairer question: which genus is most typical of each cell, recorded far more often there than across the world as a whole? Then the continents come alive. In Oceania it is acacias, bloodwoods and eucalypts; in Africa figs, thorn acacias and bushwillows; in South America the ice-cream bean trees (Inga) of the rainforest and its edges; in Europe willows, spruces and birches; in North America oaks, willows, pines and junipers.

Pines are the most recorded of these genera in {{world.genus_countries.Pinus.0.1|int}}% of Spain's qualifying land and around {{world.genus_countries.Pinus.1.1|int}}% of Poland's and the United States'. Oaks lead in {{world.genus_countries.Quercus.0.1|int}}% of Spain and {{world.genus_countries.Quercus.1.1|int}}% of Mexico, which has more kinds of oak than any other country.     08 · Who records whereA map of botanists as well as trees

    Recording effort shapes every world map here. Europe and North America hold {{world.europe_namerica_share_pct|int}}% of all the plant records on land but only {{world.europe_namerica_land_pct|int}}% of the land. The tropics are {{world.tropics_land_pct|int}}% of the land and hold {{world.tropics_records_pct|f1}}% of the records, though they are home to most of the world's tree species. Europe has {{world.continents.Europe.records_per_1000km2|int}} records per 1,000 km²; Africa has {{world.continents.Africa.records_per_1000km2|int}}.

    So I keep a cell only when it has {{world.min_records|int}} or more plant records, and fade it when it has fewer than ten times that. That leaves {{world.cells_ok_land_pct|int}}% of the world's land, and {{world.continents.Africa.cells_ok_pct|int}}% of Africa. Switch to "Recording effort" to see the gaps. In the heart of the Congo basin only {{world.gaps.Congo basin.ok_pct|int}}% of cells pass, with a median of {{world.gaps.Congo basin.median_records|int}} records; in the inner Amazon {{world.gaps.inner Amazon.ok_pct|int}}%; in central Siberia {{world.gaps.central Siberia.ok_pct|f1}}%. These are some of the most forested places on Earth, and the emptiest on this map.

    How I did this

    Woodland. The National Forest Inventory (NFI) GB 2024 woodland map, layer NFI_GB_IFT_Data_20250826, has {{nfi.polygons|int}} polygons ({{nfi.woodland_polygons|int}} woodland, the rest open ground inside woods). I had each polygon's centroid, interpreted forest type, country and area, from two downloads that together give the full count with no duplicates. I group the fourteen woodland types into seven: broadleaved; mixed, mainly broadleaved; mixed, mainly conifer; conifer; young trees; felled and restocking (felled, ground prepared, windblow, failed); and other (low density, shrub, coppice, assumed woodland). "Conifer share" is conifer and mixed mainly conifer over those plus broadleaved and mixed mainly broadleaved. For the map I draw each polygon as a disc of its own area at its centroid on a 100 m grid; county and country totals use the exact polygon areas, assigned to counties by centroid. Land areas come from ONS boundaries of ceremonial counties (England), preserved counties (Wales) and council areas (Scotland).

    Public forests. Forestry England's {{fe.subcompartments|int}} subcompartments, with main species, land use and planting year, as centroids. I class each species as conifer or broadleaf (yew and juniper as conifers).

    Records. GBIF occurrence density maps for {{world.genera_n|int}} tree genera and for all vascular plants, conifers and dicots, at about 0.088 degree resolution, retrieved on {{retrieved|raw}}. Two genera (Cedrus and Washingtonia) were matched by GBIF only to all vascular plants, so I dropped them. Each genus is mapped as its share of all vascular plant records in the cell; the world map adds cells into 0.35 degree squares. "Most typical" is the genus whose share in the cell is highest relative to its share of all the world's records (at least {{world.lq_min|int}} records of it). Species level grids for Britain were planned but were not in the download, so the British "Recorded trees" layer shows genera.

    Limits. The NFI maps woodland of 0.5 ha and over from aerial imagery, so hedgerows, small copses and lone trees are missing, and types are interpreted from the air. It covers Great Britain, not Northern Ireland. The disc drawing places area correctly to within a polygon's size but not its shape. Forestry England covers only the public estate in England, and planting years are of the current crop. GBIF counts records, not trees: it follows where people look and record, it mixes planted, garden and wild trees, and a single large survey can dominate a cell.
